# Writing in kuilt's voice

kuilt should sound like someone who knows the machinery and enjoys showing
how little of it you need. Warm, direct, compact, with a little wit. Give the
reader something to picture and a reason to keep reading.

## Start with something happening

A roomful of devices shares a pile of work. Two players want the next turn.
A phone goes through a tunnel. Start where the reader can recognise the
problem, then show what kuilt does about it. One sentence can be a story;
it need not become a cast of characters and a tour of their devices.

## Keep the character; cut the friction

Use familiar words and strong verbs. Keep a phrase like “stitches peers
together” or “now add a wrinkle” when it carries the idea. Let the weaving
metaphor earn its place; a page need not use every word in the sewing box.

Vary the rhythm. A short sentence can land a point; a longer one can carry
the thought there. Read it aloud. If simplifying a sentence makes it longer,
check whether you are explaining something the reader already understood.
Cut throat-clearing, repeated setup, and summaries that merely repeat the claim.

For example:

> A roomful of devices shares a pile of work. Now add a wrinkle: Team A paid
> for twice Team B's share. Heddle honours those shares and lends spare
> capacity to whoever needs it.

The wrinkle gives the story a turn. “Teams” needs no glossary. The last
sentence tells us why Heddle belongs in the story.

## Let the depth follow the need

Keep the entrance open to a curious non-engineer. Introduce a technical term
when it answers a question the story has raised, with a brief explanation.
Once introduced, use it precisely. Deeper pages can assume more knowledge
and carry more detail; they should keep the same direct voice.

Keep examples small enough to see the idea. Link to working setup instead
of repeating it. A good diagram can replace a paragraph.

## Be exact about the promise

Put the limit beside the claim: “A job can run twice. Merging its receipts
cannot unsend the extra email.” Keep caveats that change how someone would
use the library. Name what works today and what is still an idea.

Use this voice in guides, README files, KDoc, design docs, and contributor
notes. Scale the detail to the reader and the task. A reference entry needs
the contract; it need not retell the opening story.

[Vale's readability checks](readability.md) catch dense prose. They cannot
judge rhythm, wit, or truth. Meet the gates by removing friction, preserving
exact API names and guarantees. Before shipping, ask: can the reader picture
the problem, follow the promise, and stop without missing its limits?
